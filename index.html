Complex numbers are not imaginary

One sphere, two ways of looking at it, and a quarter-turn. That is all a complex number is.

A sphere in the complex plane

Place a ball somewhere in space. To say where its centre is, you need a position along the number line (the real part) and a height above it (the imaginary part). Together they are one complex number, z = Re + i·Im. The whole ball sits in the plane these two directions span, the complex plane.

Two views of the same ball

From the front you look straight at the complex plane. You see a circle, its centre, how far along it is and how high it is. Real part, imaginary part, everything.

From above you look down along the imaginary direction. You still see a circle, because a sphere looks round from every side. But now the height has disappeared: the imaginary part points straight at your eye. All you can read is the real part. The ball did not move. One of its coordinates simply lies along your line of sight.

That is the whole secret. The "imaginary" part is not imaginary. It is the part of the position that lies at right angles to the ordinary number line, so you only see it when you look from the side.

Distance and angle

There is a second way to say where the centre is. Stand at 0 and point at it. How far away is it? That is the modulus, |z| = √(Re² + Im²), Pythagoras. In which direction are you pointing? That is the argument: the angle between the real axis and your arm. Together they say how far out the centre is and in which direction, the same information as real and imaginary part, just in polar form.

Why i·i = −1

Press Multiply by i. The ball swings a quarter-turn around 0: its distance stays the same and its angle grows by 90°. Press it twice and the ball has turned 180°, to the opposite side of the number line. Turning half a circle is the same as multiplying by −1, so i·i = −1. No magic, just two quarter-turns.

Gauss wrote in 1831 that if +1, −1 and √−1 had been called the direct, inverse and lateral unit, instead of positive, negative and imaginary, there would hardly have been any obscurity about them.1 Lateral means sideways. With the sphere in front of you it is easy to see why.

The picture is older than the name. In 1797 the Norwegian-born surveyor Caspar Wessel presented a paper to the Royal Danish Academy, On the analytical representation of direction, in which he drew these numbers as directed lines, with the new unit perpendicular to the old one and multiplication as turning.2 It went almost unnoticed for a century. Jean-Robert Argand found the same picture independently in 1806.3

Where this leads

The same idea, one ball per prime, each touching the next, turns into a picture of the most famous open problem in mathematics: The Riemann Hypothesis, drawn with circles.
